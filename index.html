Architecture & economics

Thursday, October 22 · 1:30 to 5:00 pm

Lecture 1 · The technical architecture of AI

The bet under test

“Training compute is a good proxy for what a model can do.”

We take the AI stack apart, from chips and cloud to foundation models and applications, and look at how scaling works and where it stops working. We then compare open and closed weights.

Exercisein class · no preparation · 25 min
Using Epoch AI’s public data, find the most capable model trained below the 1026 FLOP line used by California and New York. Then ask what the rule would say about it.

In Epoch AI’s data, October 5, 202624 models above 1025 FLOP, from 14 developers. 4 above 1026.108 models released since June 2025 by OpenAI, Anthropic, Google and xAI have no public compute estimate.

Lecture 2 · The economics of intelligence

The bet under test

“Scale turns AI into a natural monopoly.”

AI combines the capital intensity of heavy industry with the increasing returns of digital markets. We look at fixed costs, prices, data, talent, and the business models firms build around them.

Exercisein class · no preparation · 25 min
The strategy game. Teams play firms and a regulator with twelve strategies, from network effects to regulatory barriers. Each team decides which to use, which to ban, and which to lobby for.

A hypothesis under pressureDemirer, Fradkin and Tadelis (2026) report that LLM inference prices fell about 1,000-fold, that open models cost about 90% less than comparable closed ones, and that no single model dominates.

Case study · Why did the open-weights bet end?

Saturday, October 24 · 1:30 to 5:00 pm
The bet under test

“Giving away model weights is a durable strategy.”

In July 2024, Meta bet on open weights. A week later, the National Telecommunications and Information Administration (NTIA) of the U.S. Department of Commerce made a regulatory bet. It recommended that the government monitor open-weight models without restricting them, and act only if evidence of risk emerged. This case study tests both bets.

Prepare with your team (Friday, October 23)
Each Canvas team has a role, usually two teams per role. Graded as part of the case-study component.
Every team brings a presentation of no more than 7 minutes, followed by about 6 minutes of questions from the instructor, and one hypothesis card for its position. The presentation answers two questions. What is your strategy? And should the government keep the NTIA approach, restrict open weights, or adopt a different rule? If you propose a rule, show its text.
  • MetaPresent the 2024 bet on open weights, what had to be true for it to work, and the indicator you would have watched. Then take the position on the NTIA approach that serves your strategy.
  • Closed-model labPresent your response to a rival that gives its weights away, and where your advantage must come from. Then say whether leaving open weights unrestricted tilts the market against you.
  • PolicymakersDecide whether open-weight models should face restrictions. Defend your rule, and name the evidence that would make you change it.
  • StartupYou build your product on open weights. Present the risks of depending on a model you do not control, and the rule you would ask the government for.

In class (times are approximate). Each team presents, then answers about six minutes of questions from the instructor meant to take you out of your comfort zone. Between presentations, the instructor gives short mini-lectures. Each one introduces a new concept and connects it to what the teams just presented. Expect to be asked whether the concept changes your position.

  2. 1:35Meta teams present
  3. 2:01Closed-model lab teams present
  4. 2:27Mini-lecture · The economics of open sourceWhy would a firm give away what it paid to build? Lerner & Tirole (2002), read against the two strategies just presented.
  5. 2:37Startup teams present
  6. 3:03Mini-lecture · Hold-upWhat happens when your product depends on an asset someone else controls? Klein, Crawford & Alchian (1978), applied to the startups’ risks and to the terms of open-weight licences.

  10. 4:04The timeline gamein class · no preparation
    Five real events are revealed one at a time. After each one, every team decides its next move in its role. Then the class compares with what actually happened and names the assumption that broke. To close, each team says whether its position on the rule survived the game.

Regulation & how rules travel

Thursday, October 29 · 1:30 to 5:00 pm

Lecture 3 · A theory of regulation for AI

The bet under test

“A rule serves the goal written in its preamble.”

Why regulate at all, who asks for regulation, and what counts as capture. We compare rules with standards, liability after harm with licensing before it, and rules that ask firms to write their own safety plans. We close with the Collingridge dilemma and what it implies for proposals to slow AI down.

Exercisein class · no preparation · 30 min
Who pays for a threshold? Each teammate holds one piece of the answer. CS students get the compute data, MBA students the revenue data, law students the legal texts. Nobody can answer alone.

Lecture 4 · How AI rules travel, and how they run

The bet under test

“A rule written in one large market ends up governing AI everywhere.”

We test the Brussels and California effects on AI, then study China’s layered approach. We work through paired cases: the same problem regulated in two places. We end with computational law and ask when compliance can actually be measured.

Three data pointsNew York amended its RAISE Act on March 27, 2026 to adopt California’s frontier thresholds.China’s rules on human-like AI companions took effect on July 15, 2026.In 2024, researchers found audit reports from 18 of 391 New York City employers under Local Law 144.

Case study · The Roosevelt Island AI Act

Saturday, October 31 · 1:30 to 5:00 pm
The bet under test

“A line drawn by law still holds once the technology moves.”

Roosevelt Island has declared itself a small independent state, and its legislature wants an AI law by the end of the afternoon. The island has a tiny market and a regulator with two staff. Every round of the hearing puts a tool from Lectures 3 and 4 to work, and the mini-lectures add new ones.

Prepare with your team (Friday, October 30)
Each Canvas team has a role, usually two teams per role. Graded as part of the case-study component.
Every team brings a presentation of no more than 6 minutes, followed by about 6 minutes of questions from the instructor, and one hypothesis card for its position. Legislators also bring their two-page draft.
  • Island legislatorsDraft a two-page law on frontier models and AI-generated content. For each provision, say whether it is a rule or a standard, and whether it acts before or after harm. One team must use a compute threshold. The other team may not.
  • DevelopersOne team plays a frontier lab, the other a startup that builds on open weights. Estimate what a compute threshold and a labelling duty would cost you, per model and as a fixed cost. Each team also receives a secret objective from the instructor and pursues it during the hearing without revealing it.
  • Island regulatorsDesign a compliance check you could run with two staff: what you would measure, with which data, and how you would spot a capable model trained below a threshold.
  • Other jurisdictionsThree teams, each speaking for one jurisdiction: California, China or the EU. Know what your jurisdiction already requires on thresholds and labels, and the conditions under which you would copy a law written by a small state.

In class (times are approximate). Each team presents, then answers about six minutes of questions from the instructor meant to take you out of your comfort zone. Between presentations, the instructor gives short mini-lectures. Each one introduces a new concept and connects it to what the teams just presented. Expect to be asked whether the concept changes your position.

The tool you keep

The hypothesis card

One page, six lines. You fill it in every session, for rules and for business strategies, and once more for the exam. Drafts save in this browser only.

Worked example

California SB 53

  1. A frontier model is one trained with more than 1026 FLOP, and the heaviest duties fall on developers with more than $500 million in annual revenue. Enacted. Targets the developer.
  2. Training compute tracks what a model can do.
  3. Few firms can afford training runs that large, so the rule reaches a handful of developers.
  4. If compute stops tracking capability, risky models below the line escape the rule, and models above it carry costs without matching risk.
  5. Models below 1026 FLOP that score at frontier level, and the share of new models with no public compute estimate.
  6. The California Department of Technology reviews the definitions every year and recommends updates to the Legislature.

By the end of the course, you will be able to

  1. Explain how an AI model is built and deployed to a reader from another discipline.
  2. Identify which features of an AI market give a firm a lasting advantage.
  3. Determine which firms a regulatory threshold covers and who bears its compliance costs.
  4. Draft a regulatory provision and justify the choice of instrument.
  5. Predict whether a rule adopted in one jurisdiction will spread to others.
