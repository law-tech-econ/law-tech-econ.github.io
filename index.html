Lecture 1 · The technical architecture of AI

The bet under test

“Training compute is a good proxy for what a model can do.”

We take the AI stack apart, from chips and cloud to foundation models and applications, and look at how scaling works and where it stops working. We then compare open and closed weights.

Lecture 2 · The economics of intelligence

The bet under test

“Scale turns AI into a natural monopoly.”

AI combines the capital intensity of heavy industry with the increasing returns of digital markets. We look at fixed costs, prices, data, talent, and the business models firms build around them.

Exercise25 min
The strategy game. Teams play firms and a regulator with twelve strategies, from network effects to regulatory barriers. Each team decides which to use, which to ban, and which to lobby for.

A hypothesis under pressureDemirer, Fradkin and Tadelis (2026) report that LLM inference prices fell about 1,000-fold, that open models cost about 90% less than comparable closed ones, and that no single model dominates.

Regulation & how rules travel

Thursday, October 29 · 1:30 to 5:00 pm

Lecture 3 · A theory of regulation for AI

The bet under test

“A rule serves the goal written in its preamble.”

Why regulate at all, who asks for regulation, and what counts as capture. We compare rules with standards, liability after harm with licensing before it, and rules that ask firms to write their own safety plans. We close with the Collingridge dilemma and what it implies for proposals to slow AI down.

Lecture 4 · How AI rules travel, and how they run

The bet under test

“A rule written in one large market ends up governing AI everywhere.”

We test the Brussels and California effects on AI, then study China’s layered approach. We work through paired cases: the same problem regulated in two places. We end with computational law and ask when compliance can actually be measured.

The tool you keep

The hypothesis card

One page, six lines. You fill it in every session, for rules and for business strategies, and once more for the exam. Drafts save in this browser only.

Worked example

EU AI Act, Article 51

  1. General-purpose AI models trained with more than 1025 FLOP are presumed to have high-impact capabilities. Enacted. Targets the model.
  2. Training compute tracks what a model can do.
  3. Few firms can afford training runs that large, so the rule reaches a handful of frontier developers.
  4. If compute stops tracking capability, risky models below the line escape the rule, and models above it carry costs without matching risk.
  5. Models below 1025 FLOP that score at frontier level, and the share of new models with no public compute estimate.
  6. Article 51(3) lets the Commission amend the threshold by delegated act.

  5. Predict whether a rule adopted in one jurisdiction will spread to others.

Grading & workload

Two grades, one final mark

Grades follow Cornell Law School’s grading policies. The goal is for as many of you as possible to understand the subject in depth and earn an excellent grade.

40%

Team case studies

Two presentations, on Saturday October 24 and Saturday October 31. Teams of four or five mix law, MBA and CS students and are formed by the instructor. Every member of a team receives the same grade. Online students work in online teams.

60%

Individual take-home exam

An individual case study, taken online, in which you combine your legal skills with the economic and technical insights from the course. The hypothesis card is the method to use. The registrar sets the date: check My Exam Information. Grading is anonymous, so do not write your name or any identifying information in your answers. A mock exam is posted a few days before. You receive written feedback and have one week to discuss your grade.

Using AI tools

Cornell’s Code of Academic Integrity treats any AI assistance not permitted in writing as unauthorized.

  • Take-home exam. The exam instructions state in writing whether AI tools are allowed. The Law School’s take-home instructions for Cornell Tech courses also apply. Without written permission, AI assistance is not allowed.
  • Team presentations. AI tools are permitted. State which tools you used and for what, and check every source yourself. A fabricated citation is a violation of the Code.
  • The 2031 card on October 31. Written without AI and without devices.
